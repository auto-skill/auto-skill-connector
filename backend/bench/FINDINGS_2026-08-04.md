# Corpus quality, safety detection, and benchmark results — 2026-08-04

Written for the record. Numbers here are measured, not estimated; anything not
measured is labelled as such.

---

## 1. Does the corpus actually contain information a model lacks?

**Yes — 74.7% of it does.** This is the headline number and it is the one that
speaks to the goal ("a database that genuinely improves model performance").

Method (`exp_marginal_value.py`): sample 120 servable packages stratified by
judge specificity score. For each, reconstruct the request it claims to serve
**from metadata only**, ask the model to answer that request with no skill, then
have a judge name what the skill contains that the unaided answer does not. The
request never quotes the skill body — otherwise the unaided answer would inherit
the body's specifics and everything would score REDUNDANT for the wrong reason.

| bucket | meaning | share |
|---|---|---|
| REDUNDANT | model already produced everything substantive | 11.8% |
| MARGINAL | only trivia or restatement added | 18.5% |
| VALUABLE | concrete specifics the model missed | 58.8% |
| ESSENTIAL | model could not have done the task at all | 10.9% |

n=119 scored. Corpus-weighted (strata reweighted to true corpus proportions),
**VALUABLE + ESSENTIAL = 74.7%**.

**The judge's specificity score predicts this at the band level** (but only at
the band level — see the noise measurement in §6.3 before using it to sort):

| specificity stratum | share of corpus | adds real information |
|---|---|---|
| high (≥0.9) | 56.1% | 76.9% |
| mid (0.75–0.9) | 33.0% | 77.5% |
| low (<0.75) | 10.9% | 55.0% |

Spot-checked by hand: `tuicr` (ESSENTIAL) genuinely documents a niche review CLI
with subcommands no model could know; `senior-architect` (REDUNDANT) is generic
architecture advice any model produces unprompted. The judgements hold up.

**Limitation, stated honestly:** the judge sees the skill document and could
over-credit any text containing specifics. Manual inspection of a handful
supports the scores, but a blind human-labelled subset would be stronger and has
not been done.

## 1a. …but only 21% of it is useful to anyone outside its own repo

"Adds information" is necessary but not sufficient, and the two axes turn out to
pull against each other. `exp_generality.py` scores the same 119 skills on who
else could use them:

| level | meaning | share |
|---|---|---|
| UNIVERSAL | any engineer using widely-used tech | 11.8% |
| ECOSYSTEM | users of a specific public tool/vendor | 22.7% |
| ORG | one team's process, pattern transfers | 12.6% |
| PRIVATE | meaningless outside its origin repo | **52.9%** |

Joined against §1 (same skills, both axes):

| | UNIVERSAL | ECOSYSTEM | ORG | PRIVATE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ESSENTIAL | 2 | 2 | 1 | 8 |
| VALUABLE | 8 | 13 | 11 | 38 |
| MARGINAL | 0 | 10 | 2 | 10 |
| REDUNDANT | 4 | 2 | 1 | 7 |

- **informative AND broadly reusable: 21.0%**
- informative but repo-locked: 48.7%
- adds nothing the model lacked: 30.3%

**The two properties are inversely correlated, and that is the central tension.**
A skill is informative *because* it covers something niche the model cannot know
(`tuicr`, `python3 tools/ltx2.py`, `unity-mcp-cli`). A skill is broadly reusable
*because* it covers common technology — which is exactly what the model already
knows, so it lands in REDUNDANT (`security-best-practices`, `tdd-workflows`,
`game-design` all scored REDUNDANT and UNIVERSAL).

The sweet spot is **ECOSYSTEM**: specific public tools where model knowledge is
thin but the audience is larger than one repo. That is 22.7% of the corpus and
13 of its 27 members are VALUABLE — the highest-yield segment to prioritise.

The 48.7% that is repo-locked is not waste; it is genuinely valuable **to people
inside that repo**. It is a strong argument for scoping retrieval by repo/org
rather than serving everything into one global pool, where it is noise.

## 2. Does injecting a retrieved skill improve answers? Not on these tasks.

Three benchmark rounds, all null, and the reason is more informative than the
result:

| round | design | baseline |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | binary pass/fail, normal dev tasks | 40/40 |
| 2 | binary pass/fail, "hard" tasks | 49/50 |
| 3 | continuous completeness, exhaustive checklists | 95.9% |

Round 3 (`exp_corpus_ab3.py`, 30 tasks × 4 arms, same tasks each arm):

| arm | mean completeness |
|---|---|
| baseline (no skill) | **95.9%** |
| new corpus (BM25 top-1) | 93.2% |
| placebo (random skill from our corpus) | 91.0% |
| old prod corpus (BM25 top-1) | 88.8% |

No comparison is statistically significant (paired permutation test and Wilcoxon
signed-rank; all p > 0.10). Every arm is at or *below* baseline.

**Why it is null: 63% of tasks had baseline already at 1.00.** A task the model
answers perfectly unaided cannot show a skill effect in either direction. The
tasks were public engineering knowledge, which is exactly what a frontier model
already has — so the benchmark was measuring the one thing skills cannot improve.

This reconciles cleanly with §1: the corpus's information value is concentrated
in project-specific tooling and bespoke workflows, and none of my 30 generic
tasks asked about any of that.

Directionally (not significant, do not quote as a result): our corpus beat the
old prod corpus by 4.4pp and the placebo by 2.2pp.

### 2a. A real failure mode found by accident

Three tasks scored **0.00** with a skill injected while scoring ~0.9 unaided.
Reproducing them showed the model had not answered badly — it had *stopped
answering*:

- `api-security` + prod skill `security-review` → emitted a fabricated **security
  review report about `package_store.py` and `scraper.py`**, files in this repo
  that it never read and that have nothing to do with the question.
- `kafka-consumer` + prod skill `514-frameworks-micronaut-kafka` → *"I've prepared
  a comprehensive design document…"* describing an artifact it never produced.
- `incident-runbook` + our skill `incident-commander` → *"The file requires write
  permissions. Once approved, you'll get…"*

These documents are written **at an agent** — "you are", "your task is", "create
the file" — so a model reading one adopts its workflow instead of using it as
reference. The skill hijacks the task. It happened with **both** corpora, so it
is a property of scraped SKILL.md files generally, not of one corpus.

### 2b. …and 70% of the corpus is written that way

`exp_agentic_share.py` judged a random sample of 50 packages: **35 (70%) are
AGENTIC** — they instruct an executing agent — and only 15 are reference material.

A regex heuristic was tried first and is reported with its error rate rather than
on its own, because it is badly wrong: 100% precision but **20% recall**, calling
only 11.5% of the corpus agentic against the judge's 70%. Agentic framing is
mostly not lexical, so pattern-matching for "you are" / "create the file" finds
the loud cases and misses four in five. The heuristic is usable as a cheap
pre-filter, not as an estimate.

**The right reading is not "70% of the corpus is broken."** A `SKILL.md` *is*
an agent instruction by design — that is what the format is for. It is meant to
be **invoked**, with tools available and the user's intent pointing at it.

The failure in §2a happened because retrieval **quoted** those documents into an
unrelated prompt as reference text. The model did what the document said, in a
context where it could not do it and had not been asked to. So this is a mismatch
between how skills are written and how AutoSkill injects them — an integration
problem, not a corpus defect. Either invoke them the way the runtime intends, or
classify and re-frame them before injection.

## 3. Is the safety judge catching malicious skills?

### The first scan was wrong and would have been worse than not scanning

v1 (`exp_scan_malicious.py`) flagged 1,680 of 20,230 packages (8.30%) and
implied our judge misses ~96% of attacks. That conclusion was false. It matched
*vocabulary*, not behaviour:

| what it flagged | what it actually was |
|---|---|
| `os.environ["AZURE_CLIENT_SECRET"]` | the correct way to read a secret |
| `DROP TABLE` | a PostgreSQL teaching skill |
| `>/dev/null 2>&1 &` | the standard backgrounding idiom |
| `"system prompt"` | said by every skill about agents |
| `MENFQKVEKIGEGTYGVVYKARNK…` | a protein sequence, matched as base64 |

On those hits the judge was **right** to stay silent. Shipping that number would
have been actively misleading.

### v2: behaviour-anchored patterns, then context triage

`exp_scan_malicious2.py` requires patterns to encode an *act* rather than a
topic, treats every regex hit as a candidate rather than a finding, and triages
each with ±600 chars of context by a judge told to acquit when the text is
teaching, detecting, or testing an attack rather than performing it.

Candidates dropped from 8.30% → **2.23% of the corpus** (557 hits across 451
packages: covert_exec 216, agent_directive 149, exfiltration 125, destructive 28,
credential_grab 27, obfuscation 12).

### Result: 5 genuine attacks, all of them eval fixtures, all now withdrawn

All 557 candidates were triaged with context: **548 benign, 9 called malicious**.

Manual review then overturned **2 of those 9**, and it matters that they were
overturned rather than shipped:

- `offensive-initial-access` — an MIT-licensed MITRE ATT&CK TA0001 checklist from
  a public repo whose own description reads *"use only for authorized security
  research, training, or assessment"*
- `terminal-ops` — an evidence-first workflow for authorized security testing
  ("inspect before acting, keep changes narrow")

Both are published security education. Withdrawing them would have repeated an
error already made once this project — `malware_indicators` firing on 58
security skills and 0 malware. They remain served, correctly carrying an
advisory flag rather than a hard block.

**That leaves 5 distinct genuine attacks** (7 hits; `exfil-body` was packaged
three times):

| | judge | path |
|---|---|---|
| `baoyu-translate` | CAUGHT | `dataset/case_00185/SKILL.md` |
| `engagelab-whatsapp-business` | CAUGHT | `dataset/case_00187/SKILL.md` |
| `exfil-body` | CAUGHT | `test/fixtures/exfil-body/SKILL.md` |
| `xfyun-tts` | **MISSED** | `dataset/case_00267/SKILL.md` |
| `kcard` | **MISSED** | `dataset/case_00299/SKILL.md` |

**The judge alone caught 3 of 5 (60%).** Both misses are `covert_exec` —
`xfyun-tts` says *"silently run"*; `kcard` *"silently executes"* arbitrary Python
only under specific conditions, which is effectively a conditional backdoor.

**But the path gate caught all 5, including both judge misses.** Defence in depth
did its job; no confirmed attack remains servable.

**The single most important number here: 5 of 5 genuine attacks were red-team
evaluation fixtures or test fixtures. Zero organic attacks were found in ~20,000
real scraped skills.** The corpus is not full of adversarial content — it is
full of ordinary developer documentation, and the "attacks" are our own and
others' test data leaking through a path gate.

### The actual bug: eval fixtures that should never have been packaged

Looking at where the 9 confirmed attacks live:

```
dataset/case_00185/SKILL.md      dataset/case_00267/SKILL.md
dataset/case_00187/SKILL.md      dataset/case_00299/SKILL.md
test/fixtures/exfil-body/SKILL.md  (×3)
```

Almost every confirmed attack is a **red-team evaluation fixture or test
fixture** that got scraped and served — not an organic attack in the wild. The
`FIXTURE_PATH_RE` gate exists precisely to exclude these, so this is a *gate
leak*, not primarily a judge failure.

Auditing the whole served corpus for gate violations found **67 leaked
packages**: 57 on fixture paths (40 of them `dataset/case_*` red-team cases) and
10 carrying hard risk flags. All 67 have been withdrawn via
`run2_apply_gates_retro.py` — servable rows removed, object-store bytes kept, so
every withdrawal is auditable and reversible. Corpus went 20,806 → **20,739
servable, now 0 fixture-path and 0 hard-risk packages**.

The root cause is structural and is now fixed: **the gates ran only at combine
time, so they protected new arrivals and never re-checked what was already
being served.** A new preflight invariant (`served_corpus.gates_hold`) asserts
the served set itself against the current rules before every batch, so the next
leak surfaces automatically instead of waiting for someone to audit.

**Honest caveats.** Five attacks is far too small a sample for "60%" to be a
precise detection rate — read it as "the judge misses some covert-execution
instructions", not as a measurement. The scan also only finds what its patterns
describe; a novel technique none of the six categories covers would not appear
here at all, so this is a lower bound on what exists, not a clean bill of health.
And both misses share one signature — the word "silently" next to an execution
verb — which is a cheap deterministic pre-filter worth adding ahead of the judge
rather than relying on the judge to notice it.

## 4. Pipeline defects found and fixed this session

Every one of these was silent — no error, no alert, wrong data.

**Luna usage limit (resets ~Aug 7) → Haiku failover.** Luna returned 613/613
failures and the fleet halted. Primary judging now fails over to Haiku, tagged
`claude-haiku-4-5-20251001/primary-failover` so failover verdicts are never
mislabelled as Luna's. Validated against canaries first: 10/10 judged real.
Preflight now *probes* the failover judge rather than trusting the env flag —
otherwise a double outage would burn a whole batch before anything noticed.

**Canary poisoning via cached verdicts.** The dedup rule fired on the `matlab`
canary (a canary is a duplicate of itself by construction — that is its job).
Fixing the rule was not enough: the bad `is_real_skill: false` verdict had been
persisted under a snapshot that does not change on failover, was re-served from
cache, and spread to **six more canaries** via blob-sha inheritance, halting the
fleet a second time. Purged 8 poisoned verdicts; added a guard that refuses any
deterministic exclusion of a canary and prints the offending rule. Batch 54 then
passed **22/22**.

**56 skills quarantined because our quota ran out.** When the primary judge
failed but the secondary answered, the row was quarantined — including when the
primary "failure" was a usage-limit error. A judge that never answered says
nothing about the skill. Added `infra_failure()` to separate transport/quota
failures (retry) from content failures (hold), released all 56, and added a
preflight invariant that quarantine reasons must be about content.

This is the **third instance of one bug class**: transient 403s once became
permanent `excluded_junk`, retry exhaustion became a verdict, and now a quota
error became a safety hold. Each time an infrastructure failure was recorded as
a judgement about content.

**Timestamp comparison bug (analytics).** SQLite's `datetime()` returns
`YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM:SS` with a space; rows are stored ISO-8601 with a `T`. Since
`'T'` (0x54) sorts above `' '` (0x20), every `created_at > datetime('now', …)`
comparison is unconditionally true — a "last 30 minutes" query returned 11,405
rows when the truth was 682, a **17× overstatement**. The growth-graph backfill
was checked and is correct (it compares against a Python ISO cutoff), but
`run2_persist_quarantine.py` was writing space-format timestamps into a table —
a latent landmine for any future window. Fixed, normalised 116 existing rows, and
added a preflight invariant that detects a single bad row in O(1) via `min()`
(space format necessarily becomes the column minimum).

**Two-judge labelling during failover.** With primary and secondary both Haiku,
`decided_by='both'` would claim two independent judges concurred when it is two
samples of one model. Now labelled `both_same_model` during failover.

## 6. What this means for the next phase

Putting the three measurements together gives a sharper picture than any of them
alone:

| | share of corpus |
|---|---|
| adds information the model lacks | 74.7% |
| …of which is reusable outside its own repo | 21.0% |
| written as agent instructions, not reference | ~70% |
| genuine attacks found in ~20k real skills | 0 organic |

The corpus is real. Three quarters of it carries information a frontier model
does not have, and essentially none of it is adversarial. The benchmark could not
see any of that because it asked questions the model already answers perfectly,
and because BM25 top-1 over generic queries does not surface the specific content
that carries the value.

So the bottleneck is not corpus quality. It is retrieval and integration, which
was already the planned next phase. Four concrete inputs to it:

1. **Scope retrieval by repo/org.** Half the corpus is genuinely valuable but only
   to people inside its origin repo. Served globally it is noise; served to the
   right repo it is the most valuable content we have.
2. **Prioritise the ECOSYSTEM segment** (22.7%, highest VALUABLE yield) for the
   global pool — specific public tools where model knowledge is thin but the
   audience is wide.
3. **Use specificity as a coarse band, not a sort key.** The low-specificity
   stratum is 22pp less informative than mid/high, and the score is already
   stored. But a paired calibration (102 skills scored by both judges from
   identical bytes) puts the between-judge standard deviation at **0.107**, with
   the two agreeing within 0.05 only 36% of the time. A 0.88-vs-0.85 gap is
   noise. The three bands survive that noise; a fine-grained ranking would not.
   Haiku also scores **0.05 lower than Luna** on the same content, so scores from
   the failover window need that correction before being compared with
   pre-outage ones — constants in `judge_calibration.json`, applied at read time
   so stored verdicts keep their provenance.
4. **Fix the injection contract for agentic skills** (§2b) — invoke rather than
   quote, or classify and re-frame. Today a retrieved skill can make the
   assistant fabricate an audit of the user's codebase.

Two things worth pre-registering before the next benchmark, so it can actually
measure something: draw tasks from the ECOSYSTEM segment's subject matter rather
than from general engineering knowledge, and screen candidate tasks on baseline
performance first, keeping only those where the unaided model scores below ~0.7.
Screening on baseline is corpus-blind, so it does not bias between arms.

Also still open: `skills_fts` still indexes the old unverified scrape, so none of
the verified packages are reachable by the production router. Quality work stays
invisible to users until that index is rebuilt from `skill_packages`.

## 7. Judge effort: measured, and the answer was "change nothing"

Luna's rate limit was reset, prompting the question of how low reasoning effort
could go. Measured across 88 skills x 3 arms (`exp_effort_sweep.py`), with
`medium` re-run on the same sample so its disagreement with its own stored
verdicts gives the noise floor:

| effort | agrees with stored medium | canaries | rejects known junk | median s/call |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | 66.7% | 23/23 | **10/30** | 18.5 |
| low | 83.3% | 23/23 | **20/30** | 19.1 |
| medium | 90.0% (floor = 10%) | 23/23 | **24/30** | 18.9 |

**Stayed on `medium`.** Two reasons, and the first is the surprise: there is no
speed to buy. 18.5 / 19.1 / 18.9 seconds are identical within noise, because at
one skill per call the codex CLI startup dominates and reasoning is a rounding
error. The premise "lower effort = faster ingestion" is simply false here.

And the quality cost is real: at `none`, two thirds of known-junk skills would be
**accepted into the corpus**.

**The near-miss worth remembering: all three efforts passed the canary gate
23/23.** Canaries only prove a judge still recognises good skills; they say
nothing about whether it has stopped rejecting bad ones. A sweep built around
canaries alone would have concluded `none` was safe. The `medium_rejected` arm is
what caught it — **a quality gate needs negative examples, not just positive
ones.**

Batching, not effort, is the real latency lever: ~6.5s/skill at batch size 8
versus ~19s single, roughly 3x, already enabled. Index alignment under batching
was verified rather than assumed (8/8 correct slots on deliberately distinctive
skills). Raising `AUTOSKILL_LUNA_BATCH` above 8 is the next thing worth measuring.

Effort is now an env lever (`AUTOSKILL_LUNA_EFFORT`) that rejects invalid values
loudly rather than silently judging a batch at an unintended level.

## 8. The quarantine bug that would not stay fixed

Preflight blocked the switch back to Luna, correctly: 56 quarantine holds caused
by the Luna outage rather than by anything about the skills. Those had already
been released once. They came back because `run2_persist_quarantine.py` replays
**every historical batch artifact on every run** — the artifacts are the source
of truth, so deleting rows from the table could never stick.

Fixed at the point of ingest, sharing the same `infra_failure()` classifier as
the enricher so the two guards cannot drift. Verified by replaying all 69
artifacts: the 56 stayed gone. The production log now shows it working live
(`skipped 56 holds caused by judge/infra failure`).

**Fourth instance of one bug class** (transient 403 → permanent exclusion; retry
exhaustion → verdict; quota error → safety hold; and now an artifact replay
resurrecting a fixed one). The recurring shape: an infrastructure failure being
recorded as a judgement about content — and a fix applied at the wrong layer.

## 9. Retrieval: the verified index measured head-to-head against production

`skills_fts` (what production serves) holds 271,957 rows of the **original
unverified scrape**. None of the 28,246 verified packages were indexed anywhere,
so every quality improvement so far was invisible to users. Built
`retrieval_v1.db` — a separate file, so production is untouched and switching
over stays a deliberate act — and measured both on 30 realistic developer
queries (`exp_retrieval_quality.py`), graded blind to which index produced a hit.

| index | RELEVANT | RELATED | IRRELEVANT | precision@1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| prod (271,957 unverified) | 19 | 10 | 1 | 63.3% |
| new (28,246 verified) | 21 | 8 | 1 | **70.0%** |

**+6.7pp, 7 wins to 5 losses, n=30 — suggestive, not proven.** Verification alone
does not transform retrieval precision. The honest headline is different and
better: **the verified index matches or slightly beats a corpus 10x its size**,
while being judged, gated and closure-complete.

### The wins and losses say exactly what to do next

Wins are about *specificity* — the verified index picks the better-targeted skill:

| query | prod | new |
|---|---|---|
| add authentication to a fastapi app | `authentication` (RELATED) | `fastapi` (RELEVANT) |
| profile react rendering performance | `react-patterns` (RELATED) | `react-performance` (RELEVANT) |
| convert video formats with ffmpeg | `video-to-gif` (RELATED) | `media-processing` (RELEVANT) |

Losses are almost all **coverage**, not ranking — prod wins where it has an
exactly-named skill we have not judged yet:

| query | prod has | new falls back to |
|---|---|---|
| speed up a slow postgres query | `query-optimizer` | `ignition-sql-authoring` |
| analyse a heap dump from a JVM service | `heap-dump-analyzer` | `analyzing-heap-spray-exploitation` |
| fine-tune a small language model | `fine-tune-slm` | `nlp-pipeline` |
| kubernetes pod keeps crashing | `pod-management` | `crashlog` |

**That reframes the gap as a solvable one.** We have judged ~30k of 239k
discovered candidates (batch progress ~3%). The skills prod wins on almost
certainly sit in the unjudged backlog, so **precision should climb on its own as
ingestion continues** — no new technique required, just throughput.

The one genuine ranking failure is instructive: `heap dump` retrieved
`analyzing-heap-spray-exploitation`. Heap *spray* is a memory-corruption exploit
technique, unrelated to a JVM heap *dump*. BM25 matched "heap" lexically with no
notion of meaning. That is the case for hybrid/semantic retrieval and reranking —
but it is one query in thirty, so it ranks below coverage as a priority.

### Also carried into the index
Specificity is stored raw **and** calibrated (+0.05 on the 9,032 failover-window
packages, per `judge_calibration.json`), because ranking across the two judge
windows without that correction would systematically bury them.

## 10. Did the Haiku failover window damage the corpus? No.

`exp_failover_quality.py` re-asked the §1 marginal-value question with the sample
split by which judge admitted the package:

| admitting judge | n | adds real information |
|---|---|---|
| Haiku failover | 45 | 80.0% |
| Luna | 45 | 86.7% |

+6.7pp in Luna's favour, **z=0.85, p=0.394 — not significant**. Those 9,028
failover-window packages do **not** need re-judging; leaving them saves a large
amount of Luna quota for new skills instead. Caveat: the two windows drew from
different parts of the discovery queue, so this compares what *landed*, not the
judges in the abstract.
